02 / The theory behind it

Four conditions make a deadlock possible.

In the classic operating-systems model, all four conditions must hold at the same time for a deadlock to occur.

CONDITION 01

Mutual exclusion

A resource can be held by one transaction at a time when its lock is exclusive.

CONDITION 02

Hold and wait

A transaction keeps a lock it already owns while waiting for another resource.

CONDITION 03

No preemption

A lock is not simply taken away; it is normally released by the transaction or recovery process.

CONDITION 04

Circular wait

T1 waits for T2's resource, while T2 waits for a resource held by T1.

03 / Getting things moving

Detect it. Break the cycle. Continue.

Database engines differ in their exact policies, but the general idea is to detect the conflict and make a transaction release its locks.

⌕

1. Detect the cycle

A database can use a wait-for graph or another detection method to find transactions waiting in a loop.

↶

2. Roll back a victim

The system chooses one transaction to abort or roll back, releasing its locks so the cycle is broken.

✓

3. Resume safely

The other transaction can proceed. The rolled-back work may be retried, depending on the application.

04 / Where it matters

A real-world problem, not just a textbook diagram.

Databases sit behind banking apps, online shopping, ticket booking and inventory systems. When concurrent operations need the same records in different orders, lock conflicts can slow or interrupt work.

This example is deliberately simplified to make the lock cycle visible. Real database systems have additional safeguards and different recovery strategies.
